# Blog
Throughout this course, I will write posts about data science topics I am interested in.
## Blog 1
Data science is often framed as a purely technical domain—a realm of clean pipelines, fast algorithms, and optimized models. But when the data we analyze is generated by human behavior, technical skill alone isn't enough. Modeling social topics matters because numbers allow us to observe societal patterns that would otherwise remain invisible. Through data, we can track systemic healthcare inequities, analyze urban resource distribution, or observe how information spreads across communities. By modeling human interactions and social systems, we move from vague intuition toward concrete evidence, giving us the tools to address complex real-world challenges with greater accuracy.

However, using data derived from human lives carries profound ethical weight. Behind every row in a dataframe, point on a scatter plot, or weight in a neural network is a real person with rights, privacy, and real-world consequences tied to the outcomes of those systems. Data is never neutral; it inherits the biases, historical context, and power dynamics of the environment in which it was gathered. Treating people's data strictly as raw computational input risks automating discrimination, reinforcing systemic inequities, and breaching trust. As practitioners, our fundamental responsibility is to interrogate our datasets, audit models for bias, protect user privacy, and remain relentlessly critical of the assumptions built directly into our code.

This is precisely why studying data science within its social context matters. Technical mastery in tools like Python means very little if we lack the social literacy required to interpret what the outputs actually mean in human terms. Through this course, I hope to better understand how to design technical workflows that actively account for human context. I want to learn how to ask better foundational questions before collecting data, recognize the hidden blind spots in human-centered datasets, and develop models that respect human complexity rather than flattening it. Data science shouldn't exist in a vacuum; it gains its true value when grounded in human reality.
